Working example, built around Trailer Made, LLC's own published facts

A 30-day mobile-kitchen build shouldn't go quiet, and a defect shouldn't surface at delivery

Trailer Made's own homepage markets a structured 30-day build process for its Chef Supreme mobile-kitchen trailers: a Day-0 strategy call, days 1-25 of construction with photo and video check-ins, and a Day-30 customer walkthrough approval before final payment is collected. Its BBB record shows that promise breaking down under a real order queue: one customer who paid $15,000 upfront went six months past a 12-week estimate with no proactive update, and a separate $44,530.83 commercial-kitchen-trailer customer received their unit over two months late with defects, wrong oven placement, unusable sinks, faulty wiring, leaks, discovered only at delivery. Trailer Made's own BBB response says it absorbed over $6,500 in unbilled labor, parts, transportation and electrician costs fixing this after the fact. Below is a working tool sized to close exactly that gap: a stage timeline the customer can check anytime, a required, logged update the moment a promised check-in or delivery date slips, and a Day-30 walkthrough step where the customer explicitly approves the trailer or logs a defect, before final payment is requested, not after.

Everything below is fictional. Customer names, order details, dollar amounts and dates on this page are sample data, illustrative only, not Trailer Made's real customer or order records. Sample order TM-1001 is deliberately shaped to mirror the "no proactive update while a promised date slips" pattern described in Trailer Made's own BBB complaint record, and TM-1004's resolved defect note mirrors the "defect found at delivery" pattern from the $44,530.83 complaint, recast here as caught and fixed before the walkthrough is approved rather than after, neither reflects a real Trailer Made customer.

How this gets built for real

The order number is whatever Trailer Made already uses. A real build reads from the existing deposit confirmation or work order number instead of inventing a new one for the customer to remember.
The notification pings here are log entries, not real sends. A real build wires the check-in, exception and walkthrough pings to Trailer Made's existing info@trailer-made.com Google Workspace inbox, so an update reaches the customer the moment staff log it, matching the "photo and video check-ins" it already promises.
"Confirmed" here is a typed name and a timestamp. A real build can add an actual e-signature provider for a stronger record on a schedule slip or the Day-30 walkthrough approval; this demo shows the workflow, not a specific vendor.
The Day-30 gate blocks final payment, not just a status label. A real build ties the walkthrough-approved state to whatever actually triggers the final invoice, so a flagged defect holds the payment request the same way it holds the demo's "Delivered" stage here.
This does not fix a fabrication defect by itself. It only makes a schedule slip or a walkthrough defect visible and logged the moment it happens, instead of surfacing six months late or only at delivery.
