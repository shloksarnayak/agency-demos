The rules underneath

These are the part worth arguing with. When more than one fires on the same ticket, the most restrictive action wins, that's why SAMPLE-4419 is human-send-only even though Rule 1 would otherwise have it acknowledged inside 48 hours.

01
WhenNothing has gone out within 48 hours of a ticket arriving, or, for anything landing on a Friday, by end of the following Monday.
ThenSend an acknowledgment carrying the order's actual current status, not a "we received your message" note. Assign a named owner. Keep it flagged overdue until a person has touched it.
Your own FAQ tells customers you'll get back to them within 48 hours, and that Friday inquiries get a reply the following Monday. This doesn't invent a standard, it makes the one you already published the thing the system enforces. Missing a response time you set yourself is the easiest sentence in the world for an unhappy customer to write, because they can screenshot your page next to it.
02
WhenA second message arrives from the same customer about the same order, with no reply in between.
ThenUrgency moves up one level, the ticket jumps ahead of newer mail, and the queue label reads "2nd contact, unanswered" so nobody has to open it to know what it is.
In the public complaint record, repeat contact with no reply is attached to nearly every other issue rather than standing on its own, customers describing four emails, six emails with documentation, one describing roughly a hundred. By the time someone writes a formal complaint, the count of ignored attempts is usually the story, not the original problem. Counting repeat contact is enough to break that pattern.
03
WhenAn order has a shipping confirmation, but no carrier scan has been recorded for 7 calendar days since the label was created.
ThenMove to critical. Queue the order for a physical check at the fulfillment location. The reply states plainly that no scan has been recorded and that we're checking physically, it does not ask the customer to allow more time.
Your shipping information tells customers tracking may take up to 48 hours to update after a label is created. Past days rather than hours, "please allow a little more time" stops being true and the customer can see that on their own screen. Note what this rule deliberately does not say: a missing scan isn't proof the package never entered the network, USPS does accept parcels it fails to scan. Asserting otherwise in writing is a claim a later scan can embarrass you with.
04
WhenA ticket crosses 7 days since the first unanswered contact, or the message contains BBB, chargeback, dispute, attorney general, lawyer, "scam", a named review site, or a threat to post publicly.
ThenNo automated reply goes out at all. The system drafts and holds; a named person with authority to approve a reship, refund or goodwill gesture must send it the same day.
Public complaints about your business tend to surface somewhere around the two-to-three week mark. I can't prove silence is what causes that escalation, it's a pattern across review sites, not a mechanism I've tested, but it's the one variable sitting on your side of the line. And once someone has typed "BBB", another automated note reads as more of the same. That's why this rule takes the ticket away from the automation rather than speeding it up.
05
WhenThe reply would have to communicate a decision the published policy either refuses or doesn't cover: a package with a delivered scan, a final-sale item, or a prize-value complaint with no published claim path. (A ticket where the reply only routes and asks, like SAMPLE-4420, isn't deciding anything, so this doesn't catch it.)
ThenThe system does not send the decision. It assembles the evidence, full tracking history, scan detail, order contents, what the relevant policy actually says, drafts the reply, and holds it for a person to approve, edit or overrule.
These are the cases where speed alone makes things worse. A quoted policy landing in four minutes reads as a machine refusing you. The automation's job here is the gathering and the drafting, never the deciding, and there's a straightforward commercial argument that a goodwill call on a $280 order costs less than a permanent public complaint about one.
06
WhenAny product-issue ticket, damaged, broken, wrong or missing item, regardless of how calm the customer sounds.
ThenLock the inbound timestamp to the ticket and flag it time-critical. Route to the matching replacement form where one exists (broken candle; damaged or wrong-size jewelry) and to a named fulfillment owner where I found none (short shipment, wrong item). A ticket needing both gets split, not queued behind one path. Tell the customer in writing whether their report landed inside the 48-hour window, and where it didn't, flag for a person before any window is quoted.
Three separate 48-hour clocks are already live on your pages: product issues reported within 48 hours of receipt, replacement candles shipped within 48 hours of the broken-candle form, replacement jewelry within 48 hours of that form. A polite email with photos is the easiest one in the inbox to leave until tomorrow, and tomorrow is where all three quietly break.
07
WhenA follow-up date promised to a customer passes with nothing sent.
ThenThe ticket goes straight back to critical and to the owner who made the promise, ahead of everything else in their queue.
Every draft on this page commits to a date. A system that manufactures written, dated callback promises with nothing enforcing them doesn't fix a non-response problem, it generates fresh evidence of one, in your own words, with a timestamp. This rule exists because the rest of the page would be dangerous without it.
08
WhenMore than [your threshold] tickets in the queue are older than seven days at once.
ThenA daily aging digest goes to a named manager: every overdue ticket, its age, the customer's ask in one line, and whether anything has gone out. New tickets keep flowing normally, the digest is the escalation.
On the public review record, three separate grievances logged across roughly two and a half months were all answered on the same day, with near-identical text. From the outside that reads like a catch-up sweep, though you'd know what actually happened that week. Either way, a sweep always arrives too late for whoever waited longest. A short daily list is what stops the pile forming, and it costs a manager about ninety seconds a morning.

The thing I'd fix before buying any software

This came out of reading your policy pages closely enough to write replies that quote them. I'd rather hand it over than keep it as a sales point.

Your published policies currently contradict each other, and automation would multiply whichever version it's pointed at.

  • Two processing windows on the same page. /pages/refund-policy states "All orders are processed + shipped within 2-3 business days" and, further down, asks customers to "apply our 3-5 business day window for processing." No reconciling text.
  • Three return windows, live simultaneously. 60 days from purchase date (/policies/refund-policy), 30 days from receiving your order (/pages/refund-policy), and 60 days after receiving your order (/pages/faq).
  • A terms page that contradicts all of them. Section 3 of /pages/jewelry-candles-terms-and-conditions: "All sales are final once completed and any refunds, exchanges, or any related resolutions come at the sole discretion of Jewelry Candles LLC."
  • Two different toll-free numbers appear in otherwise near-identical policy text on different pages.
  • The page carrying your shipping timeframes isn't linked from your own navigation. I checked the raw HTML of the footer, homepage and both FAQ pages and found no link to it. It's still a live URL, I'm not claiming customers can't reach it, only that they're unlikely to land on it from your site.

This is why I kept returns out of all four samples. A rep can't answer consistently when the source doesn't. Reconciling that text is a prerequisite, not a nice-to-have, and it's your text to reconcile, not a vendor's. It's also free.

What this demo can't do

The parts a proposal would normally leave out.

The integration is the product; the wording is just what makes it worth opening. These replies say things a demo cannot know, that no scan has been recorded since the label, that a delivery scan happened at a specific time, that four of five items shipped. Every one of those is only true in production with live read access to your order data and to USPS and UPS tracking. Without that, all four collapse into a polite acknowledgment, which is something that can be sent with no integration at all.
I don't know what you already run. I didn't research your current helpdesk, autoresponders or order-status notifications, and I'm not claiming you have none. A vendor describing a gap he hasn't verified is a vendor guessing. If something's already in place, the useful question is whether these rules sit on top of it or replace it.
I can't tell which inbox this would watch. Three customer-facing email addresses and two different toll-free numbers appear across your own pages, and /pages/contact-us is a Wufoo form embed rather than a helpdesk. Triage pointed at the wrong queue is worthless, so the first real question isn't technical, it's which of those actually lands somewhere a person works.
The system reads text, not photographs. On SAMPLE-4420 it counted two attachments; it did not assess whether the crack is visible in them. Your broken-candle form is explicit that you won't replace a candle without a picture of the actual breakage, so a machine pre-approving photos would set customers up to be rejected later by your own reviewer. Counting is safe. Judging isn't.
Two drafts assume a business decision, not a software one. SAMPLE-4418's bracketed paragraph, and the bracketed clause near the end of SAMPLE-4417, both offer to open a carrier trace as the sender. Whether you're willing to do that is your call, and your published policy currently points customers to the carrier themselves. If the answer is no, those come out and both replies get noticeably weaker, worth deciding deliberately rather than discovering by default after it's built.
Every sample here is a bad week. All four score medium or higher, because I built them from public complaints, which are by definition the ones that went wrong. A real week has a long tail this doesn't show: tracking questions, ring-size changes, restock asks, appraisal follow-ups. That tail decides whether triage saves time or just adds a step, and I have no visibility into your ticket volume, staffing or current response times. Anyone quoting you a percentage improvement without those three numbers is making it up.
The complaint text is paraphrase, not quotation. Tone is modelled on publicly posted complaints and reviews, but everything I read passed through a summarizing step. If a line sounds like a specific customer you recognise, that's coincidence, not a record.
I'd build this to draft and hold rather than send unattended, particularly where the honest answer is no. A four-minute automated decline is worse than a slow human one. If you want fully unattended sending, that's a different design with different risk, and I'd want to argue about it with you before building it rather than after.
